Chapter 1

Four layers, one planet

Earth is built like a peach with a hard stone at its heart: a thin outer skin, a thick middle and a dense core.

Throughout this VegaCore guide we will use these four layers as a map. Heat left over from Earth’s formation and from radioactive decay keeps the interior hot, and that heat drives almost everything you will read about next.

Chapter 2

How do we know what we cannot see?

Nobody has drilled even a fraction of the way to the centre, so scientists use indirect clues. The most powerful clue is sound.

Seismic waves

Earthquakes send waves through the planet. Their speed and path reveal what they passed through.

The liquid clue

Shear waves cannot cross liquid. Because they vanish on the far side, we know the outer core is liquid.

Rock samples

Volcanoes carry fragments of the mantle upward, giving direct samples of deep rock.

Magnetism

Earth’s magnetic field points to moving metal deep inside, as in a giant dynamo.

Chapter 3

Plate tectonics

Earth’s crust and the top of the mantle are broken into large pieces called tectonic plates. They drift on the softer rock below at a few centimetres a year, roughly the speed at which fingernails grow.

Where plates pull apart, new crust forms. Where they collide, mountains rise or one plate sinks beneath another. Where they slide past each other, stress builds along faults. Over millions of years this slow motion has opened oceans and raised the Himalayas.

Chapter 4

Earthquakes

An earthquake is the sudden release of stored stress when rock along a fault slips. The point underground where it starts is the focus, and the spot directly above is the epicentre.

Chapter 5

Volcanoes

A volcano is an opening where molten rock, called magma, reaches the surface and is known as lava. Most volcanoes sit near plate boundaries, and a famous belt around the Pacific is called the Ring of Fire.

Shield volcanoes

Broad, gentle slopes built by runny lava, as in Hawaii. Eruptions are usually calm.

Stratovolcanoes

Steep cones of layered ash and lava, such as Mount Fuji. Thicker magma can make eruptions explosive.

Geysers and springs

Water heated underground rises to the surface, creating geysers, hot pools and steaming vents.

Chapter 6

The invisible shield

Swirling liquid metal in the outer core generates Earth’s magnetic field. This field stretches far into space and deflects much of the charged particle stream flowing from the Sun, helping protect the atmosphere and everything that lives under it.

Deep inside the planet, moving metal makes a shield that reaches into space.

The field is also what makes a compass needle point north, and it creates the glowing auroras near the poles when solar particles meet the upper air.

Chapter 7

Discover New Options In Geothermal Energy

Earth’s internal heat can be used as a clean, steady energy source. In places such as Iceland, hot water from underground is piped into homes for heating, and steam from deep wells spins turbines to produce electricity.

Unlike sunshine or wind, geothermal heat flows day and night. New technology is also making it possible to tap hot rock in more locations, not only near volcanoes. VegaCore readers often find this the most surprising link between deep geology and daily life.

Chapter 8

Explore What’s Trending In Earth Science

3D seismic imaging

Researchers combine thousands of earthquake recordings to build detailed pictures of the mantle, revealing giant hot plumes and sunken slabs.

Smarter quake detection

Machine-learning programs now sift through seismic data to find tiny tremors that human analysts might miss.

Fibre-optic sensing

Ordinary telecom cables can act as long lines of sensors that feel ground movement over many kilometres.

Inner-core studies

Scientists continue to study how the solid inner core grows and rotates, which helps explain the magnetic field’s history.

Chapter 9

Find Something Better: Building For Earthquakes

We cannot stop earthquakes, but good design can save lives. Engineers have developed several ways to help buildings survive shaking.

Flexible structures

Steel frames and reinforced concrete can bend slightly without breaking, absorbing the energy of shaking.

Base isolation

Buildings rest on flexible bearings that let the ground move while the structure above stays much calmer.

Strong connections

Walls, floors and roofs are tied firmly together so that they move as one unit.

Early warning systems

Sensors detect fast waves and send alerts seconds before slower, stronger waves arrive, enough to stop trains and take cover.

Questions people ask

Could we drill all the way to the centre?

Not with present technology. The deepest hole ever drilled, in Russia, reached about 12 kilometres, and rising heat and pressure stopped further progress.

Is the centre of the Earth as hot as the Sun?

The inner core is estimated at roughly 5,000 to 6,000 degrees Celsius, similar to the Sun’s visible surface, though the Sun’s core is far hotter.

Can earthquakes be predicted?

Not precisely. Scientists can estimate where earthquakes are likely and how large they might be, but cannot name the exact day and time.

Why is the inner core solid if it is so hot?

The pressure at the centre is so immense that the iron is squeezed into a solid state despite the extreme temperature.

The Ground Beneath You Is Alive

Our planet is still moving, heating and changing, even if we rarely notice it. We hope this VegaCore guide gives you a new way to think about the ground you stand on.
